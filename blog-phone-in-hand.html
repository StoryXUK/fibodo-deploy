Phone in Hand, Business in Motion

We spend a lot of time worrying about young people and technology. Sometimes rightly. But what if the phone in their hand is not only a distraction? What if, with the right skill and the right support, it is also the first tool of a working life?

This is the part I keep coming back to.

We should be encouraging more young people to work for themselves.

Not in some empty "be your own boss" motivational poster way. Not in the polished LinkedIn sense, where everyone apparently gets up at 4:30am, drinks mushroom coffee and has seven passive income streams before breakfast.

I mean in a practical, grounded, slightly messy way.

Because many young people are already halfway there.

Give a 13-year-old a phone and they can edit video, build an audience, sell clothes, run a group chat, promote something, take feedback, make content, understand trends, test a message, spot what lands, and move on before most businesses have finished their first internal meeting about "Gen Z engagement".

That should make us pause.

The Reality Facing Young People

Because in education, employment and wellbeing, we are having several conversations at once.

We are worried about young people spending too much time on screens. Fair enough. The phone is already in their hand. The question is whether we help them use it to scroll, or to build something.

That is not a soft point. It is an employability point.

We are worried about social media being addictive, manipulative and, frankly, weird. Also fair enough.

And we are worried about young people being locked out of work.

Young people are being told to get skills, be resilient, be entrepreneurial, adapt to change, protect their mental health, find purpose, build confidence and prepare for jobs that may not exist yet.

Then they are walking into a labour market where entry-level work is harder to find, work experience has thinned out, AI is starting to eat the bottom rungs of some career ladders, and the old promise — study hard, get the job, climb the ladder — no longer feels quite as reliable as it once did.

That should bother every educator, parent, employer and policymaker.

Because if the first rung disappears, how does anyone climb?

Technology Isn't the Problem

This is where I think we need a more useful conversation about technology.

Not the lazy one where technology is either saving the world or ruining childhood.

Both things can be true.

A phone can be a dopamine vending machine.

It can also be a camera, editing suite, booking system, shopfront, payment terminal, learning device, portfolio, community hub and customer service desk.

The same tool that can trap attention can also build opportunity.

The question is not whether young people should use technology.

They already do.

The question is whether we help them use it to create something healthier than endless scrolling.

Modern Business Skills Are Already Being Learned

There is an uncomfortable truth here.

We live in a world where people make money from photographs of their feet, cat memes, reaction videos, gaming commentary, digital templates, resale accounts, unboxing clips, niche newsletters, AI prompts, online tutoring, fitness plans, comedy sketches and videos of people organising their fridge.

Some of it is brilliant. Some of it is nonsense. Some of it is exploitative. Some of it is surprisingly entrepreneurial.

But it tells us something important:

  • Money has moved.
  • Work has moved.
  • Attention has moved.
  • Trust has moved.
  • Opportunity has moved.

And young people have noticed.

They are not waiting for permission in the way previous generations did.

Promotion. Audience. Offer. Feedback. Iteration. Consistency. Community. Payment. Reputation.

Those are business skills.

And right now, too many of those skills are being developed in unstructured, unsupervised and often extractive environments.

What If We Did Things Differently?

What if we took the digital confidence many young people already have, added a real-world skill, wrapped it in proper safeguarding, education and support, then gave them a responsible way to earn?

That is not naïve.

That is probably one of the more realistic routes into the future of work.

Look at what motors did to agriculture. Look at what computers did to business.

The work did not disappear. It changed shape.

Now we are entering another shift.

The clever ones will not be the people who choose between "in person" and "technology".

The clever ones will combine them.

A Real-World Example

Let's make this practical.

Let's say Kendall is 13 and loves dance.

Right now, Kendall already understands clips, sound, comments, group chats, trends, edits, captions, shares, how quickly people swipe away, and how fast a bad idea dies online.

A few years later, Kendall trains properly. Earns a recognised qualification. Adds CPD or a microcredential in youth dance, group instruction, movement coaching or working with beginners.

In the old model, Kendall waits.

  • Waits for a studio to offer hours.
  • Waits for a school to notice.
  • Waits for a gym to give space.
  • Waits for a recommendation.
  • Waits for a job that may not be there.

In the new model, Kendall can start building.

  • Run beginner dance classes.
  • Offer one-to-one lessons.
  • Create on-demand tutorials.
  • Host livestream sessions.
  • Build a paid community.
  • Run holiday workshops.
  • Partner with local venues.

That is not fantasy.

That is a small business.

And perhaps more importantly, it teaches:

  • How to make an offer.
  • How to price your time.
  • How to communicate clearly.
  • How to handle bookings.
  • How to deliver what you promised.
  • How to take feedback.
  • How to build trust.
  • How to earn.

Those are not side-hustle skills.

They are life skills.

The Opportunity Ahead

Young people are not just being damaged by technology.

Some are being trained by it.

Not always in ways we would choose, but they are learning speed, communication, creativity, instinct, editing, distribution and audience behaviour.

Education should not ignore that.

It should civilise it.

It should take that raw digital fluency and connect it to proper skills, real standards, community value and safer ways to earn.

The future will not only reward people who know things.

It will reward people who can learn, adapt, combine tools, communicate value and build trust.

A Business in Motion

This is not about telling every young person to become an entrepreneur.

Some will want employment. Some will need employment. Some will thrive in teams, organisations, public services, clubs, schools, studios, charities, gyms and leisure centres.

Good.

We need all of that.

But we also need to stop treating self-employment as something people fall into when the "proper" route fails.

For many young people, especially in wellness, fitness, coaching, dance and community activity, self-employment or portfolio work may be the route.

So let's prepare them properly — with real skills, sensible tools, safeguarding, mentors, business basics, routes to customers, and education providers who understand that qualification is not the end of the journey.

The phone is already in their hand.

The question is what we help them build with it.

A distraction machine?
Or a business in motion?
